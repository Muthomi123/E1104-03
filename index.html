The Consent Gap

We click ‘accept’ dozens of times a week. Do we really control what happens to our personal data?

Before you read on, can we personalise your ads?

We and 847 partners would like to use cookies to understand you better.

Rejecting personalised ads costs £5 a month

Keep reading free by accepting, or subscribe to refuse tracking.

This banner is a demonstration. Nothing is tracked or charged.

This project tests whether the ‘consent’ we give online amounts to real control. It combines the UK Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) Public Attitudes survey of 8,019 UK adults (ICO, 2025) with my own audit of five platforms’ privacy policies and cookie banners. It asks three questions:

  1. How aware are people of data collection and of their data rights?
  2. Does consent, as currently designed, produce real understanding and control?
  3. Who bears the cost of the consent gap, and can regulation close it?

Research question 2

Clicking ‘yes’ is not the same as understanding

Data protection law treats consent as the moment a person takes charge of their data. To be valid, it must be freely given, specific and informed. The ICO’s own survey suggests that, in practice, the moment rarely produces understanding or control.

When asked about ‘consent or pay’ choices, where a website asks users to accept tracking or pay a fee, almost nine in ten respondents claimed at least some understanding. Yet 42% admitted they were not entirely sure what the options meant, and fewer than one in five felt either informed or in control.

The gap between 89% and 19% is the consent gap.

The emotions people report are telling. Scepticism (24%) was more common than feeling informed, and many felt pressured (17%) or frustrated (16%). Draper and Turow (2019) call this ‘digital resignation’: people go along with data collection not because they agree with it, but because they believe resistance is futile. Consent becomes a ritual rather than a decision.

Research question 2

The price of saying no

In theory, consent means being able to refuse. In practice, refusal increasingly has a price tag. Since the ICO confirmed in 2025 that ‘consent or pay’ models can be lawful, many UK news sites have adopted them. In my audit, the Guardian lets readers continue free only if they accept all cookies. Refusing personalised advertising requires its £5-a-month Ad-Lite subscription (Fox, 2025). Meta now offers UK users a no-ads subscription at £2.99 a month on the web and £3.99 in its apps (Gollasch, 2025).

Faced with this choice, people overwhelmingly accept.

Srnicek (2017) shows how platforms rely on cross-subsidisation: a service is offered ‘free’ to one group because another group, advertisers, pays for access to their data. ‘Consent or pay’ makes this hidden bargain visible. It puts a price on privacy, and in doing so confirms the critical political economy claim that users’ attention and data are the real commodity (Fuchs, 2017). For a student or a low-income household, a £5 subscription is not a neutral alternative. Privacy becomes something people buy, rather than a right everyone holds.

My audit, research question 2

Nearly two hours of small print

Informed consent assumes people can actually read what they agree to. On 6 November 2026, I measured the privacy policies of five platforms that UK adults use daily: Google, the Guardian, TikTok, Instagram and Amazon. I recorded each policy’s length, its estimated reading time at an average adult reading speed of 238 words per minute (Brysbaert, 2019), and its Flesch Reading Ease score.

None of the five reaches the plain English threshold. TikTok and Amazon score at roughly university reading level. Reading all five policies once would take nearly two hours, and McDonald and Cranor (2008) estimated that reading every privacy policy a typical internet user encounters would take around 201 hours a year. It is unsurprising, then, that most people do not read them: Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch (2020) called the claim to have read terms ‘the biggest lie on the internet’.

The banner results are revealing for a different reason. After pressure from the ICO, rejecting cookies now usually takes one click, the same as accepting. On the surface, the interface problem that Nouwens et al. (2020) documented across European sites appears to have been fixed. But the burden has moved rather than disappeared. It now sits in the length and difficulty of the documents behind the banner, in visual nudges that make ‘reject’ less prominent, and in the price attached to refusal. The banner is compliant; the gap remains.

Research question 1

Knowing your rights is not using them

The ICO tracks public awareness of information rights as a measure of its own performance, and the headline figures look encouraging. Three quarters of UK adults know at least one of their rights, and awareness of the right to be informed when data is collected rose from 44% to 48%.

Source: ICO (2025).

Reading the data critically, awareness and control are not the same thing. Only 16% know all of their rights. Even those who do still face the same long policies and priced refusals. Solove (2013) argues that ‘privacy self-management’, the model in which individuals protect themselves by reading notices and making choices, cannot work at the scale of modern data collection. Measuring success through awareness keeps responsibility with individuals, a pattern Foucault’s work helps us see: people are governed by being made responsible for managing themselves. Rising awareness alongside persistently low control (19%) suggests the problem is structural, not a lack of knowledge.

Research question 3

Who gets left behind

The consent gap is not shared equally. Alongside its main online survey, the ICO interviewed 500 people with very low or no digital familiarity by telephone. Their responses show that those least able to navigate consent are among the most worried, and the least protected by knowledge of their rights.

As more public and commercial services move online, people are drawn into data collection whether they wish to be or not. Lyon (2001) describes surveillance as social sorting: it affects groups differently depending on their resources and position. Here, the people most exposed to datafication are those with the fewest tools to question it.

Research question 3

The rules keep moving

Regulation has narrowed some gaps while widening others. Yellow markers show changes that strengthened users’ position; red markers show changes that loosened it.

  • May 2018: UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 make clear, informed, freely given consent a legal standard.
  • Late 2023: The ICO warns major websites that rejecting cookies must be as easy as accepting them.
  • April 2024: The Guardian adds a one-click ‘No, thank you’ to its banner.
  • January 2025: ICO guidance confirms ‘consent or pay’ models can be lawful if certain conditions are met.
  • March 2025: The Guardian launches Ad-Lite: refusing personalised advertising now costs £5 a month.
  • June 2025: The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 receives Royal Assent.
  • September 2025: Meta introduces a paid no-ads subscription for UK Facebook and Instagram users.
  • February 2026: The Act’s cookie changes take effect: some analytics and functionality cookies no longer need prior consent, though users must be told and able to opt out. Maximum fines for breaking the cookie rules rise sharply.
  • June 2026: The Act is fully in force.

The direction of travel is ambiguous. Stronger enforcement and the ‘reject all’ standard have improved banners. At the same time, regulators have accepted paid refusal and reduced the number of cookies that need consent at all. Advertising and cross-site tracking still require consent, but the overall effect is a model in which consent is easier to give, costlier to withhold, and needed less often.

Conclusion

Mind the gap: what this tells us

Across all three questions, the evidence points the same way. People are increasingly aware of data collection and of their rights (RQ1). But consent, as currently designed, does not translate that awareness into understanding or control (RQ2), and the gap falls hardest on those with the fewest resources (RQ3). The consent gap is not a failure of individual users. It is built into a data economy that depends on extraction.

Data extraction

Sadowski (2019) defines extraction as taking data without meaningful consent or fair compensation. A one-click ‘accept’ behind two hours of unread policy meets the letter of consent, not its meaning.

Platform capitalism

Srnicek (2017) and Van Dijck, Poell and de Waal (2018) show platforms are fuelled by data. ‘Consent or pay’ reveals the price of opting out of that fuel supply.

Privacy self-management

Solove (2013) argues that individual choice cannot carry the weight placed on it. The audit shows why: the reading burden alone makes informed choice unrealistic.

Digital resignation

Draper and Turow (2019) explain why people accept despite distrust. The ICO’s mix of scepticism and acceptance fits this pattern closely.

Five steps to close the consent gap

  1. Standardise the small print. Require short, plain-English summaries in a common format, so policies can be compared at a glance rather than read for two hours.
  2. Keep refusal affordable. Regulators should scrutinise the price of ‘consent or pay’, so privacy does not become a premium product.
  3. Measure control, not just awareness. The ICO should track whether people feel and are in control, and treat the 19% figure as a key indicator.
  4. Protect offline routes. People with low digital engagement need ways to use essential services without being drawn into data collection.
  5. Shift from individual clicks to collective enforcement. Proactive regulation and collective complaints place responsibility on platforms rather than users.

Method and limitations

How this project was done

Secondary data. Survey figures come from the ICO’s 2025 Public Attitudes report: an online survey of 8,019 UK adults (10 January to 21 February 2025), weighted to the UK online population, plus a telephone survey of 500 people with low or no digital engagement. The data is self-reported, so it captures perceptions rather than behaviour. The report’s executive summary gives the ‘caution’ figure as 52% while the main text gives 51%, a reminder to read such figures carefully. A 2026 edition has since been published, which future work could use to track change.

Primary data. On 6 November 2026, I audited five platforms. I copied each UK-facing privacy policy and calculated its word count, reading time (238 words per minute) and Flesch Reading Ease score. I tested each cookie banner in a private browser window through a UK VPN connection, counting the clicks needed to accept and to reject all non-essential cookies. Where a site’s refusal option involved payment, I confirmed the details against published sources.

Limitations. Five platforms is a small sample, and policies and banners change frequently. Flesch scores are designed for continuous prose and are indicative for structured documents. Instagram’s figures were measured on the Instagram data policy page dated January 2022, which may differ from the current Meta Privacy Policy. Banner tests reflect one browser on one day.
